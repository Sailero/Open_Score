# 1 Introduction

Consider an attack–defense mission with three geographically separated critical assets. The attacking team must breach at least two assets before a deadline, while the defending team must preserve at least two. Units are lost in combat and reinforcements arrive during the same episode. A centralized commander on each side must repeatedly decide how many and which active units to send to each front; the assigned agents then act through decentralized low-level policies. A single-agent decision rule cannot resolve this resource trade-off: an agent can fight well locally while the team still loses because too many units were committed to one front and too few to another.

Hierarchical command is not itself new. Structured prediction has combined learned assignment scores with centralized optimization and generalized from small training instances to substantially larger cooperative problems [@carion2019structured]. COPA coordinates dynamic team compositions through a global coach [@liu2021copa]. ALMA learns high-level subtask allocation and low-level execution, and finds that joint training can outperform separately trained components [@iqbal2022alma]. Hierarchical air-combat and command-and-control systems have likewise separated commander decisions from executor control [@selmonaj2023hierarchical; @c2opponent2026]. Recent work has further addressed graph-based dynamic interception allocation and adaptive UAV alliances [@jia2026dtgat; @dynamicalliance2026]. Competitive coalition formation is also established: Double-IC couples intra-team coalition formation with inter-team competition [@duan2025twolevel].

The remaining question is therefore not how to add another commander, graph encoder, or clustering module. It is how the commander should evaluate a candidate temporary team. A learned critic estimates policy return but is not necessarily calibrated as a mission-success probability. A hand-crafted combat score can ignore nonlinear interactions between geometry, health, time, task, and the fixed executor. A supervised predictor trained only on allocations selected by an old commander may be accurate on logged actions yet fail precisely on the unexecuted allocations explored by a new optimizer. The optimizer then amplifies systematic overestimation: it preferentially selects candidates that look unusually good because the model is wrong.

Related work confirms that each simple alternative is insufficient as a novelty claim. Simulation-based decision support already predicts air-combat engagement quality from pre-engagement state [@dantas2021bvr]. Capability-learning methods infer task requirements and agent capabilities from team-performance pairs before optimization [@fu2022capabilities]. Adaptive bi-level multi-robot allocation estimates task-completion probabilities and confidence bounds from execution data [@lin2025adaptive]. These methods motivate outcome-aware allocation, but they do not establish whether an intervention-trained, calibrated outcome distribution over complete bilateral coalitions improves multi-front decisions when both populations change within an episode and opponent allocation policies are held out.

We formulate this problem as an open-population competitive coalition Markov game. Each side maps its active set to front–task tokens at a macro time scale. A shared, task-conditioned lower-level policy executes the resulting local subgames for a fixed horizon. Death and reinforcement change the active sets, triggering replanning. Assignment itself defines the coalition: agents assigned to the same front and task form a subteam, so no separate geometric clustering objective is required.

Our working method, COGAR, inserts an auditable execution interface between the two levels. We clone the same simulator snapshot, intervene on multiple complete red–blue allocation pairs, and use common random numbers to roll them out under a frozen executor. An Execution-Grounded Outcome Model predicts distributions over success, losses, time, and asset progress. A risk-aware macro solver evaluates bilateral candidate allocations, penalizes uncertainty and reassignment, solves a regularized matrix game, and executes one receding-horizon decision. Two rounds of optimizer-aware data aggregation target candidates that the current solver rates highly or for which ensemble members disagree.

The paper is organized around three falsifiable hypotheses. First, paired interventions and calibration should reduce false-safe predictions and Monte Carlo selection regret, including after the commander has selected a candidate. Second, this reliability should improve asset-level mission outcomes under population and event shift while all upper-level methods share the same executor, observation, candidate set, and compute budget. Third, bilateral risk-aware allocation should improve worst-opponent performance and recovery after population events relative to fixed-opponent or hand-crafted-utility allocation.

Subject to those hypotheses being supported, the intended contributions are:

1. an explicit multi-front evaluation protocol in which both competitive teams suffer within-episode losses and receive reinforcements, with population, event, geometry, composition, and opponent-policy shifts separated at test time;
2. a same-snapshot intervention dataset and a calibrated, full-partition outcome model that predicts multi-dimensional results of unexecuted bilateral allocations under a fixed low-level policy;
3. a risk- and switching-aware receding-horizon allocation game, evaluated through post-selection calibration, false-safe rate, allocation regret, final asset outcomes, cross-play robustness, and real-time cost.

We do not claim the first hierarchical commander, dynamic grouping method, open-team policy, learned assignment value, competitive coalition game, or joint hierarchy training scheme. The contribution is the execution-grounded and decision-audited connection between local capability and macro allocation.
