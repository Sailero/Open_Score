# 1. Introduction

An adaptive game agent should respond when an opponent changes strategy. In a team battle, however, the observations used to infer that change are themselves non-stationary. Opponents are eliminated, reinforced, occluded, or replaced by units of another type. These events alter the size and distribution of the observed entity set even if every remaining opponent continues to follow the same conditional policy. An online opponent model may therefore face an elementary but consequential ambiguity: did the opponent change how it plays, or did only the opponent composition change?

This ambiguity is not resolved by a network being able to accept variable-length inputs. Permutation-invariant and permutation-equivariant (PI/PE) architectures, including UPDeT and HPN, remove arbitrary entity ordering and define policies over changing observation and entity-action sets [@hu2021updet; @hao2023hpn]. Recent population-aware methods further support joins, exits, and unseen team sizes [@guo2025shppo; @li2026gira]. These properties make computation structurally valid after attrition, but they do not identify which part of a temporal representation encodes composition and which part encodes opponent policy.

Opponent modeling addresses the complementary problem. Neural opponent representations, temporal predictors, offline in-context models, and open-ended opponent training infer behavior from interaction histories and improve responses to unseen or changing agents [@he2016opponent; @liu2022omtcn; @jing2024tao; @jing2025oeom]. Within-episode policy switches have also been studied using Bayesian policy reuse and online change detection [@chen2022bayesokr; @hernandezleal2014switching]. Consequently, neither temporal context nor switch detection alone is a sufficient novelty claim.

The closest recent work jointly models fluctuating opponent numbers, evolving intentions, strategies, and world trajectories with a hypernetwork state-space model [@cheng2026hyperjd2tssm]. This demonstrates that variable populations and dynamic intentions can be handled together, but leaves a narrower identification question untested: whether a learned strategy representation moves because the strategy changed or merely because the represented population did. Joint prediction can perform well while still exploiting composition shortcuts. Without independently intervening on composition and policy, aggregate win rate cannot reveal this failure.

We therefore study **composition--strategy confounding** in team-competitive partially observable Markov games. We construct four within-episode event classes by crossing a composition intervention with a policy intervention: neither changes, composition only, policy only, and both. The protocol exposes false strategy-change alarms, switch detection delay, and the decision cost after a true switch. It also permits a direct falsification: if generic opponent contexts remain stable under composition-only events, the proposed problem is not empirically important in that environment.

Our proposed method is deliberately small. **Composition-Disentangled Opponent Context with Hyper Policy Networks (CDOC-HPN)** retains an HPN-style PI input and PE entity-action output. It uses an instantaneous composition branch for population count, type mixture, health, and current threat. A separate recurrent branch summarizes population-normalized, state-conditional opponent actions or transitions. The policy may use both branches, but the strategy-change score uses only the behavior branch. Paired training trajectories regularize behavior codes from the same policy under different compositions to be close, while separating different policies under matched compositions. No opponent identifier is provided at test time and adaptation requires no gradient update.

The distinction matters for decisions. A composition-aware policy should immediately react to fewer threats; a strategy model should not interpret that physical change as evidence that the surviving opponents adopted a new tactic. Conversely, it should detect a switch from focus fire to kiting even when the unit count remains constant. Separating these two responsibilities aims to reduce needless response changes after attrition without suppressing genuine adaptation.

Our intended contributions are:

1. **A falsifiable diagnostic problem and protocol.** We define composition--strategy confounding and independently intervene on team composition and opponent policy, measuring composition-only false alarms, policy-switch AUROC and delay, and post-switch regret.
2. **A lightweight representation.** We propose CDOC-HPN, which separates instantaneous composition from recurrent conditional behavior and trains the latter with paired cross-composition invariance and matched-composition separation objectives.
3. **Decision-level evidence.** We test whether improved identification translates into lower post-switch regret and stronger held-out-opponent performance in public MAgent2 Battle and a controlled two-team environment, with capacity and latency controls. **[Replace with measured findings after the frozen five-seed study.]**
